Two official series tell us how building prices are moving in Great Britain, and in 2026 they are telling slightly different stories. The Department for Business and Trade’s (DBT) material price index for “All Work” was 6.4% higher in August 2026 than a year earlier. The Office for National Statistics’ (ONS) construction output price index, which tracks the price of finished work rather than the materials that go into it, showed 1.9% annual growth in the 12 months to June 2026. This post sets out what each figure measures, why they differ, and what they look like next to a calculator band for a typical extension and loft.

What the material price figures say

DBT’s monthly building materials commentary for September 2026 (published 7 October 2026, covering August data) reports the All Work material price index up 6.4% year on year and up 0.4% on the month. In July the annual rate was 5.9%, so the pace picked up slightly over the summer.

The rise was not even across the industry. By type of work, the material index for new housing was up 5.6% on the year, other new work 7.4%, and repair and maintenance 5.9%. Repair and maintenance is the closest of the three to the kind of jobs this site covers, such as extensions, lofts, kitchens and heating upgrades on existing homes.

Some individual materials moved much further than the average:

  • fabricated structural steel: up 21.2% year on year
  • rigid pipes and fittings: up 15.5%
  • concrete reinforcing bars (rebar): up 12.5%

Others were slightly cheaper than a year earlier:

  • ready-mixed concrete: down 0.7%
  • cement: down 0.8%
  • imported sawn wood: down 1.6%

The same commentary reports deliveries of bricks down 10.1% and concrete blocks down 6.5% compared with a year earlier. Deliveries are a volume measure, not a price, but they give a sense of how busy the building trade is.

What the construction output price figures say

The ONS publishes construction output price indices (OPIs) every quarter. They aim to measure the price of the construction work itself — what clients pay for finished output — rather than the cost of inputs. In its bulletin released on 13 August 2026, the ONS reported annual output price growth of 1.9% in the 12 months to June 2026.

On activity, the ONS estimate for August 2026 (released 15 October 2026) showed construction output down 0.2% on the month and down 1.0% over the three months to August. New work fell 0.7% on the month, while repair and maintenance rose 0.4%.

Why the two numbers differ

A builder’s price is not just materials. It also covers labour, plant hire, site overheads, insurance, waste and margin. Materials can rise quickly while other parts of the price move more slowly, and in a quieter market firms may absorb some input cost rises rather than pass them on in full. The two indices also cover different periods — August for materials, June for output prices — and different scopes. So it would be a mistake to read a 6.4% rise in material prices as a 6.4% rise in a quote. Neither figure is a forecast, and this post does not attempt one.

For scale only: 1.9% of the extension mid (ex VAT) is about £1,153, and 6.4% is about £3,883. For the dormer, the same percentages are about £1,127 and £3,795. This is simple arithmetic on published percentages, not an estimate of how much any project has changed in price. Only part of a project’s cost is materials, and the share varies from job to job.

The width of the band is the bigger number. From the low end to the high end, the extension band spans about £36,400 before VAT. Specification, ground conditions, structural steel, glazing and the kitchen fit-out usually move a price far more than a few percentage points of general inflation. That is why the calculator shows a band rather than a single figure.

Where these figures might show up

Steel and pipework are the two categories with the largest rises in the DBT series. Extensions with large openings often need structural steel, and heating and plumbing work relies on pipes and fittings. If you are comparing written quotes, it can help to ask how materials are priced — for example whether a quote is fixed for a period, or whether some items are provisional sums. That is a question about how a quote is put together, not a recommendation to go ahead with any work.

Concrete, cement and imported timber were slightly cheaper than a year ago in the same series. Groundworks and carpentry make up a large share of many extensions, which is part of why the overall output price figure is much lower than the steel figure.
