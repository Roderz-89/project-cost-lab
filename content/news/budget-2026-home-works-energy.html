The Budget on 28 October 2026 did not make any headline changes to the cost of building work on an existing home. Most work on a house is still standard-rated for VAT at 20%. But several measures affect energy-saving upgrades and household energy bills, and a few touch on how homes are built. This post sets out what HM Treasury, DESNZ and HMRC have published, with a worked example on the VAT change for solar panels and insulation.

VAT on energy-saving materials returns to 5% on 1 April 2027

Since 1 May 2023, the installation of qualifying energy-saving materials in homes — including insulation, solar panels and heat pumps — has been zero-rated for VAT. HMRC’s VAT Notice 708/6 already said that this 0% rate would run until 31 March 2027, with the reduced rate of 5% applying from 1 April 2027.

The Budget confirmed that the rate will revert to 5% “as planned”. It also announced that heat batteries will be added to the list of energy-saving materials that qualify for the 5% rate from 1 April 2027, when installed in homes or charitable buildings. This will be done through secondary legislation.

At the mid, 5% VAT adds about £377 to the solar band, £41 to the loft insulation band and £101 to the cavity wall band. Taken together, the three mids are £10,395 at 0% and £10,914 at 5%, a difference of about £519.

The difference is small next to the width of each band. The solar band alone spans more than £3,300 between low and high, driven by roof layout, panel choice, scaffolding and electrics. The calculator’s VAT control lets you see a band at either rate, but the rate on a real job depends on what is being installed and how it is supplied. Heat pump grants and the Warm Homes Plan

The Budget said the government remains committed to heat pump deployment and will keep the Boiler Upgrade Scheme at current funding levels of £400 million in 2027-28, rising broadly in line with inflation after that. The current grant is £7,500 for an air-to-water or ground source heat pump. We cover the grant and VAT position in more detail in our heat pump post.

The Budget also said the government will save £1 billion of capital grant from the Warm Homes Plan, while protecting the majority of Warm Homes Plan funding for low-income households and social housing, delivered by mayors and councils. Details of how local schemes are affected are likely to come from DESNZ and local delivery bodies, and we will add them here when published.

Energy bills

Several measures affect bills, which matter when comparing the running costs of different heating systems:

  • VAT on electricity. DESNZ says VAT was removed from household electricity bills on 1 October 2026. The Budget extended this until July 2027, and expects it to take around £50 off the annual Ofgem price cap in April 2027.
  • Household Energy Payment. A £100 payment from January 2027 for households in council tax bands A to D in England, paid through the council tax system. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland receive funding for their own governments to decide on similar support.
  • Gas standing charges. The government has asked Ofgem to remove standing charges from household gas bills, as early as 2027, with targeted help for some high gas users. The costs move to the unit rate, so the effect varies by household.
  • Electricity standing charges. To be reformed, with details to be announced next year.
  • Hydrogen costs. A planned new charge on gas bills to fund hydrogen projects, the Gas Shipper Obligation, has been scrapped.

Building regulations and planning

To give housebuilders more certainty, the Budget said the government will not make major changes to building regulations for the rest of this Parliament, except to keep improving building safety, including the Grenfell Inquiry recommendations. For homeowners planning an extension or loft, the rules that apply are those already in force, set out in the Approved Documents on GOV.UK.

The Budget also announced an exemption from Biodiversity Net Gain requirements for residential brownfield developments on sites up to 1.5 hectares in England, and a new zero rate of VAT on land sold to registered social housing providers from 1 April 2027. These are aimed at housebuilding rather than work on existing homes. Under existing GOV.UK guidance, householder applications — such as extensions and loft conversions — and work carried out under permitted development rights are already exempt from Biodiversity Net Gain.

What did not change

The Budget did not change the standard 20% VAT rate that applies to most repair, maintenance and improvement work on existing homes, or the special 5% and 0% rules for certain conversions and new dwellings in HMRC’s VAT Notice 708. Planning application fees and permitted development rules are set separately by the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government (MHCLG), not through the Budget.

The Office for Budget Responsibility published its Economic and fiscal outlook alongside the Budget. It sets out the official forecasts behind the Budget; we have not drawn any project-cost estimates from it.
