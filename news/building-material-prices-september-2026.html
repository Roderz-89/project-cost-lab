Building material prices up 7.2% in a year: what it means next to an extension band

Published 29 Oct 2026 · Last updated 13 Nov 2026

Update due. We will update this post after DBT’s next building materials edition on 2 December 2026.

Two official series tell us how building prices are moving in Great Britain, and in 2026 they are telling slightly different stories. The Department for Business and Trade’s (DBT) material price index for “All Work” was 7.2% higher in September 2026 than a year earlier, up from 6.4% in August. The Office for National Statistics’ (ONS) construction output price index, which tracks the price of finished work rather than the materials that go into it, showed 3.2% annual growth in the 12 months to September 2026, up from 1.9% in the 12 months to June. This post sets out what each figure measures, why they differ, and what they look like next to a calculator band for a typical extension and loft.

What the material price figures say

DBT’s monthly building materials commentary for October 2026 (published 4 November 2026, covering September data) reports the All Work material price index up 7.2% year on year and up 0.6% on the month. In August the annual rate was 6.4%, and in July 5.9%, so the pace has picked up for a second month running.

The rise was not even across the industry. By type of work, the material index for new housing was up 5.4% on the year, other new work 8.5%, and repair and maintenance 6.2% (5.9% in August). Repair and maintenance is the closest of the three to the kind of jobs this site covers, such as extensions, lofts, kitchens and heating upgrades on existing homes.

Some individual materials moved much further than the average in the 12 months to September:

  • fabricated structural steel: up 19.9% year on year (21.2% in August)
  • rigid pipes and fittings: up 16.9% (15.5% in August)
  • concrete reinforcing bars (rebar): up 16.1% (12.5% in August)

Two were slightly cheaper than a year earlier:

  • ready-mixed concrete: down 0.5%
  • imported sawn or planed wood: down 1.9%

The same commentary reports seasonally adjusted deliveries of bricks down 15.4% and concrete blocks up 0.5% compared with September 2025. Deliveries are a volume measure, not a price, but they give a sense of how busy the building trade is.

What the construction output price figures say

The ONS publishes construction output price indices (OPIs) every quarter. They aim to measure the price of the construction work itself — what clients pay for finished output — rather than the cost of inputs. In its bulletin released on 12 November 2026, the ONS reported annual output price growth of 3.2% in the 12 months to September 2026. Three months earlier, in the bulletin released on 13 August 2026, the figure was 1.9% in the 12 months to June, so the annual rate of finished-work prices has picked up over the summer.

On activity, the same 12 November release estimates that total construction output fell 0.7% in July to September 2026 compared with April to June. New work fell 1.6%, while repair and maintenance grew 0.5%; the largest drag was private new housing, down 4.7%. On a monthly basis, output grew 0.8% in September after four monthly falls, including a revised 0.5% fall in August.

Why the two numbers differ

A builder’s price is not just materials. It also covers labour, plant hire, site overheads, insurance, waste and margin. Materials can rise quickly while other parts of the price move more slowly, and in a quieter market firms may absorb some input cost rises rather than pass them on in full. The two indices also have different scopes, and the output price index is quarterly while the material index is monthly. So even with both now covering the year to September, it would be a mistake to read a 7.2% rise in material prices as a 7.2% rise in a quote. The 3.2% output price figure is the closer of the two to what clients pay for finished work, but it covers all construction, not just home projects. Neither figure is a forecast, and this post does not attempt one.

With standard-rate VAT at 20% and a 10% contingency added, the extension band becomes £56,063 – £104,117 (mid £80,090) and the dormer band £54,794 – £101,760 (mid £78,277).

For scale only: 3.2% (the ONS output price rate to September) of the extension mid (ex VAT) is about £1,942, 6.2% (the DBT repair and maintenance material rate) is about £3,762, and 7.2% (the DBT All Work material rate) is about £4,369. For the dormer, the same percentages are about £1,898, £3,677 and £4,270. This is simple arithmetic on published percentages, not an estimate of how much any project has changed in price. Only part of a project’s cost is materials, and the share varies from job to job.

The width of the band is the bigger number. From the low end to the high end, the extension band spans about £36,400 before VAT. Specification, ground conditions, structural steel, glazing and the kitchen fit-out usually move a price far more than a few percentage points of general inflation. That is why the calculator shows a band rather than a single figure.

Where these figures might show up

Steel and pipework are the two categories with the largest rises in the DBT series. Extensions with large openings often need structural steel, and heating and plumbing work relies on pipes and fittings. If you are comparing written quotes, it can help to ask how materials are priced — for example whether a quote is fixed for a period, or whether some items are provisional sums. That is a question about how a quote is put together, not a recommendation to go ahead with any work.

Ready-mixed concrete and imported timber were slightly cheaper than a year ago in the same series. Groundworks and carpentry make up a large share of many extensions, which is part of why the overall output price figure is much lower than the steel figure.
