Energy price cap January to March 2027: what a 26% rise means for heat pump and boiler running costs

Published 25 Nov 2026 · Last updated 25 Nov 2026

Update due. We will update this post when Ofgem announces the cap for April to June 2027, which it says it will publish by 23 February 2027.

Ofgem announced on 25 November 2026 that the energy price cap will rise by 26% for 1 January to 31 March 2027. For a typical household paying by Direct Debit for gas and electricity, the annualised figure goes from £1,723 to £2,166, if that level were sustained for a year. Gas prices are rising by more than electricity prices, which changes the sums behind any comparison of heat pump and gas boiler running costs. This post sets out the new rates and works through that arithmetic, with a worked heat pump install band from the Project Cost Lab calculator.

The new cap in numbers

Ofgem says the rise is driven by higher international gas prices linked to the continued conflict in the Middle East. In its notes, Ofgem says gas bills rise by 33% and electricity bills by 19% in the January cap compared with October. The cap affects around 20 million households on standard variable tariffs; households on fixed deals are not affected while the fix lasts. The cap limits unit rates and standing charges, not the total bill, so what a household pays depends on how much energy it uses.

Ofgem’s average rates for a default tariff paid by Direct Debit across England, Scotland and Wales are:

Rate1 Oct – 31 Dec 20261 Jan – 31 Mar 2027
Electricity unit rate26.32p per kWh32.69p per kWh
Electricity standing charge54.83p per day54.77p per day
Gas unit rate7.97p per kWh10.96p per kWh
Gas standing charge29.68p per day29.65p per day

Gas figures include 5% VAT. Ofgem says there is no VAT on electricity from 1 October 2026 to 30 June 2027, so both columns above exclude VAT on electricity and can be compared with each other. On these averages the electricity unit rate rises by about 24% (32.69 ÷ 26.32 = 1.242) and the gas unit rate by about 38% (10.96 ÷ 7.97 = 1.375). Standing charges are almost unchanged.

Ofgem also notes that the government has announced around 20 million households will receive £100 towards their energy bill from January. Our Budget 2026 post covers what the Budget said about that payment, VAT on electricity and gas standing charges.

The electricity-to-gas price ratio

A heat pump runs on electricity and a gas boiler on gas, so the ratio between the two unit rates is the starting point for comparing running costs. At the cap averages:

  • October to December 2026: 26.32 ÷ 7.97 = about 3.30. A unit of electricity cost about 3.3 times a unit of gas.
  • January to March 2027: 32.69 ÷ 10.96 = about 2.98. A unit of electricity costs about 3.0 times a unit of gas.

A heat pump costs less per unit of heat than a gas boiler when its seasonal efficiency is higher than this ratio multiplied by the boiler’s efficiency. Against a 90% efficient boiler, the break-even seasonal efficiency falls from about 2.97 (3.302 × 0.9) to about 2.68 (2.983 × 0.9). Against an 85% boiler it falls from about 2.81 to about 2.54. These are example efficiencies to show the sums, not measurements of any product. Our boiler vs heat pump guide explains what drives seasonal efficiency in practice.

Worked example: annual heating running cost at 12,000 kWh of heat

Inputs: an illustrative home needing 12,000 kWh of heat a year for space heating and hot water; a gas boiler at 90% efficiency; a heat pump at seasonal efficiency (SCOP) 2.5, 3.0 or 3.5; Ofgem’s Direct Debit cap average unit rates for each period, applied as if they held for a whole year. Energy bought = 12,000 ÷ efficiency; cost = energy × unit rate. Standing charges are left out. Figures are rounded to the nearest penny.

SystemEnergy boughtAt Oct–Dec 2026 ratesAt Jan–Mar 2027 rates
Gas boiler, 90%13,333 kWh gas£1,062.67£1,461.33
Heat pump, SCOP 2.54,800 kWh electricity£1,263.36£1,569.12
Heat pump, SCOP 3.04,000 kWh electricity£1,052.80£1,307.60
Heat pump, SCOP 3.53,429 kWh electricity£902.40£1,120.80

For the boiler at the January rates: 12,000 ÷ 0.9 = 13,333 kWh × 10.96p = £1,461.33. For the heat pump at SCOP 3.0: 12,000 ÷ 3 = 4,000 kWh × 32.69p = £1,307.60. The gap at SCOP 3.0 widens from about £10 a year at October rates (£1,062.67 − £1,052.80 = £9.87) to about £154 a year at January rates (£1,461.33 − £1,307.60 = £153.73), because gas has risen faster than electricity. At SCOP 2.5 the heat pump is still dearer to run than the boiler in both periods in this illustration.

Standing charges sit on top. At the January averages the electricity standing charge is about £199.91 a year (54.77p × 365) and gas about £108.22 a year (29.65p × 365). A household that stops using mains gas altogether may no longer pay the gas standing charge; one that keeps a gas supply for cooking still does.

The cap only sets these rates for three months, and winter heating use is higher than the yearly average, so this is arithmetic on a fixed set of prices rather than a forecast of any bill. Actual running costs depend on a home’s heat demand, the system’s real seasonal efficiency, the household’s region, meter and tariff, and how the heating is used. A heat-loss survey is what estimates a home’s heat demand.

The grant arithmetic is £10,902 − £7,500 = £3,402, £15,180 − £7,500 = £7,680 and £19,458 − £7,500 = £11,958. Without the cylinder the same settings give £9,922 – £17,638 (mid £13,780) before the grant. For comparison, a like-for-like combi boiler swap at the same settings and 20% VAT is £2,493 – £4,630 (mid £3,562). The two jobs include different work, so this is not a like-for-like comparison.

Grants and VAT: what has not changed

The Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant in England and Wales is still £7,500 towards an air source or ground source heat pump, according to GOV.UK, with an additional £1,500 until March 2027 for homes heated by oil or LPG with no mains gas connection. On 5 November 2026 DESNZ gave Ofgem permission to over-allocate vouchers to a total of £520 million in the 2026/27 financial year, “to provide certainty that vouchers will continue to be available”. Installing a heat pump in a home is zero-rated for VAT until 31 March 2027, with the 5% rate from 1 April 2027. Our heat pump grant and VAT post covers both dates in more detail.

Ofgem says it will publish the cap levels for 1 April to 30 June 2027 by 23 February 2027. The government has also asked for standing charges to be removed from gas bills in 2027 or as soon as possible afterwards, and Ofgem says it will share details later. Either could change the arithmetic above.
