Boiler or heat pump? A UK cost and suitability comparison for 2026

Last checked 27 Oct 2026

The short answer

A like-for-like gas combi swap is the cheaper job to buy and the simpler one to fit. An air-source heat pump costs several times as much before any grant, needs more space and a hot-water cylinder, and works best in a home that loses heat slowly through radiators sized for lower water temperatures. In England and Wales the Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant closes much of the gap, and until 31 March 2027 a heat pump installation carries 0% VAT where a boiler carries 20%. Whether a heat pump then costs more or less to run depends on two things you can check: the electricity-to-gas price ratio on your tariff, and how efficiently the heat pump actually runs in your house.

Installation figures below come from the calculator’s own model, compiled in September 2026. Grant, VAT, energy price and planning facts come from official sources and are dated. Any efficiency figure is an example input, not a claim about how a product performs.

For the separate cost bands for boilers, heat pumps, solar PV and batteries, and how to compare quotes, read the boiler, heat pump and PV guide. This page is the head-to-head: which to choose, and why.

The grant: Boiler Upgrade Scheme

As GOV.UK’s Boiler Upgrade Scheme pages stated when checked on 27 Oct 2026, the scheme offers one grant per property: £7,500 towards an air source (air-to-water) heat pump, £7,500 towards a ground source heat pump, £2,500 towards an air-to-air heat pump and £5,000 towards a biomass boiler. Until March 2027 there is an extra £1,500 towards an air or ground source heat pump where the property is heated by oil or LPG and has no mains gas connection. These are the same amounts the boiler guide already gives.

The scheme is open in England and Wales to owners replacing fossil-fuel heating (oil, gas, electric or LPG). The installer applies, and must install and commission the heat pump within 120 days of applying. There is no grant for a hybrid system, most new builds, social housing, or a property already funded for a heat pump or biomass boiler. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own arrangements. See GOV.UK: Apply for the Boiler Upgrade Scheme, and treat the grant as confirmed only once it is written into a quote.

VAT: 0% for a heat pump, 20% for a boiler

Under HMRC’s VAT Notice 708/6, checked 27 Oct 2026, installing qualifying energy-saving materials in a home — heat pumps included — is zero-rated until 31 March 2027 and 5% from 1 April 2027. A replacement gas or oil boiler does not qualify and is normally standard-rated at 20%. That is why the worked example uses 0% for the heat pump and 20% for both boiler options. The VAT on works guide has the detail and the exceptions; whichever option you choose, ask for the VAT rate to be shown on the quote.

Running costs: what actually drives the bill

No single “heat pumps save £X a year” figure is honest: it depends on your tariff, your house and the system design. What follows is the arithmetic with verified, dated prices, and the inputs to check.

1. The price of a unit of gas against a unit of electricity

Ofgem’s energy price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 averages 26.32p per kWh for electricity and 7.97p per kWh for gas for a default tariff paid by Direct Debit across England, Scotland and Wales, according to Ofgem’s announcement of 26 August 2026 (checked 27 Oct 2026). Gas includes 5% VAT; electricity has no VAT from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027 after the government removed it for that period. So a unit of electricity currently costs about 3.3 times a unit of gas at the cap average. Your own rates depend on your region, meter and tariff, and the cap changes every quarter; Ofgem says the January–March 2027 level will be announced by 25 November 2026.

2. How efficiently each system turns energy into heat

A boiler turns a unit of gas into slightly less than a unit of heat. A heat pump moves heat from outside air, so each unit of electricity delivers several units of heat; the ratio over a year is often called the seasonal coefficient of performance (SCOP). That ratio is not fixed: it falls when the water sent to the radiators has to be hotter and when it is colder outside, which is why design matters so much. Ask the installer for the design flow temperature and the expected seasonal efficiency in writing.

Put the two together and the break-even point is simple: a heat pump costs less per unit of heat than a gas boiler when its seasonal efficiency is higher than the electricity-to-gas price ratio multiplied by the boiler’s efficiency. The table shows the arithmetic for every 1,000 kWh of heat delivered, at the price-cap averages above. The efficiencies are example inputs to show the sums, not measurements of any product.

At those prices the break-even seasonal efficiency is about 2.97 against a 90% boiler and about 2.81 against an 85% boiler. Above that line the heat pump’s heat is cheaper per unit; below it, dearer. Multiply the gap by your home’s annual heat demand — which your installer’s heat-loss survey should estimate — to get a rough annual difference.

3. Standing charges

The same price cap sets average daily standing charges of 54.83p for electricity and 29.68p for gas, about £200 and £108 a year respectively. You pay the electricity standing charge either way. If a heat pump lets you stop using mains gas entirely — no gas hob, no gas fire — you could stop paying the gas standing charge too, but check with your supplier what closing the gas supply involves before counting that saving.

4. Tariff and timing

Some suppliers offer time-of-use or heat-pump tariffs that are cheaper at certain hours, and a cylinder can store hot water made at those times. The cap averages above do not reflect such tariffs, so compare on the rates actually offered to you.

5. The house itself

Heat demand drives both bills. Loft and cavity wall insulation reduce the heat either system has to supply, and they can let a heat pump run at a lower flow temperature. In the calculator, a 40 m² loft insulation top-up has a mid of about £907 and an 80 m² cavity wall fill about £2,216, both at 0% VAT with the default settings. Doing the fabric work first is usually the cheaper order of events, because the heat pump can then be sized for the smaller load.

6. Maintenance

Both systems need periodic servicing, and a warranty may set conditions on it. Ask each installer what the warranty requires and what a service costs, and include that in your comparison; this page does not price servicing.

Suitability: will a heat pump work in your home?

Insulation and heat loss

The installer should do a room-by-room heat-loss calculation before quoting. A poorly insulated house can still be heated by a heat pump, but it needs a bigger unit and larger emitters and is more likely to run at the hotter flow temperatures that cut efficiency. If the survey shows big losses through the loft or walls, price that work first.

Radiators and other emitters

Heat pumps work most efficiently when the water in the heating system is cooler than a typical boiler supplies. Cooler water gives off less heat from the same radiator, so some rooms may need larger radiators. The heat-loss survey shows which. Underfloor heating suits lower temperatures well, but retrofitting it means lifting floors; treat it as a separate decision, not a default.

Space indoors and out

A heat pump needs an outdoor unit with room for air to flow around it, and a cylinder indoors. A combi needs neither, so if your combi sits in a kitchen cupboard and there is no airing cupboard or loft space, finding room for a cylinder is often the first hurdle. A home with a system or regular boiler already has one.

Hot water

A combi heats water on demand. A heat pump heats a stored cylinder, so hot water is limited by the cylinder size and the time to reheat it. Large households that run several showers back to back should check the cylinder sizing with the installer.

Electrical supply

Ask the installer whether your incoming supply and consumer unit can take the extra load. If a new consumer unit is needed, the calculator’s base mid is £850 before factors and VAT.

Noise and planning (England)

Outdoor units make a fan and compressor noise, so siting matters for you and your neighbours. In England, an air source heat pump on a house is usually permitted development under Class G of Part 14 of the General Permitted Development Order, as amended from 29 May 2025 (checked on legislation.gov.uk, 27 Oct 2026), if it meets the conditions. These include:

  • it complies with the MCS Planning Standards, which include a sound calculation for the chosen position;
  • the outdoor unit is no more than 1.5 cubic metres including any housing (0.6 m³ on a block of flats);
  • one unit per house, or up to two on a detached house;
  • not on a pitched roof, and on a flat roof at least 1 metre from the edge;
  • not within the curtilage of a listed building, and in a conservation area not on a wall or roof fronting a highway;
  • sited, so far as practicable, to minimise its effect on the building’s appearance and the area’s amenity.

The old rule that no part of the unit could be within 1 metre of the boundary was removed in that 2025 change. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own rules, and a listed building or a failed sound calculation means applying for permission. Ask your installer to show you the MCS sound calculation, and check with your council if you are unsure. The region and access guide covers the site factors that also move the installation price.

Gas boilers: simpler, but not free of checks

Gas boiler work must be done by a Gas Safe registered engineer. A like-for-like swap is the least disruptive option, but moving the boiler or the flue adds cost: in the calculator, the relocation add-on takes the combi example mid from £4,231 to about £5,419.

When each one makes sense

A boiler swap tends to make sense when

  • the existing boiler has failed or is close to failing and you need heat this week rather than after a survey and design;
  • there is no practical space for a cylinder or an outdoor unit;
  • the house has high heat loss and the insulation work is not yet affordable;
  • you are in a flat or listed building where the permission route for a heat pump is uncertain; or
  • the upfront budget is the binding constraint, even after a grant.

A heat pump tends to make sense when

  • you own the home in England or Wales and qualify for the grant, ideally before the 0% VAT window closes on 31 March 2027;
  • you heat with oil or LPG off the gas grid, where the larger grant applies and the comparison is not with mains gas;
  • the loft and walls are already insulated, or you can do that first;
  • you have, or can make, room for a cylinder and a well-sited outdoor unit;
  • the installer’s design gives a seasonal efficiency comfortably above the break-even for your tariff; or
  • you are already disrupting the house — an extension, new floors or a rewire — so emitter and pipework changes cost less to add.

A boiler swap now does not rule out a heat pump later, and insulation helps either system.

Boiler vs heat pump FAQ

How much does a heat pump cost compared with a boiler?

In the Project Cost Lab model, with the default settings, a combi swap at 20% VAT has a mid of about £4,231 and a heat pump with cylinder at 0% VAT about £17,911, or about £10,411 after a £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant. These are planning ranges, not quotes.

How much is the Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant?

£7,500 towards an air source or ground source heat pump in England and Wales, with an extra £1,500 until March 2027 for eligible homes heated by oil or LPG with no mains gas connection, according to GOV.UK, checked 27 Oct 2026. The installer applies and takes the grant off the bill.

Is a heat pump cheaper to run than a gas boiler?

It depends on its seasonal efficiency and your tariff. At Ofgem’s cap averages for October to December 2026 (26.32p electricity, 7.97p gas per kWh), a heat pump beats a 90% efficient boiler on unit cost of heat when its seasonal efficiency is above about 2.97. Standing charges, tariffs and your home’s heat demand change the annual result.

Do I need planning permission for an air source heat pump?

In England it is usually permitted development if the unit meets the Class G conditions, including the MCS sound standard, a 1.5 m³ size limit and the rules for roofs, listed buildings and conservation areas. Since 29 May 2025 there is no 1 metre boundary rule. Elsewhere in the UK, check your local rules.

Is there VAT on a heat pump or a boiler?

Installing a heat pump in a home is 0% VAT until 31 March 2027 and 5% from 1 April 2027, under HMRC’s VAT Notice 708/6. A gas or oil boiler replacement is normally 20%. Check the rate on the quote.
