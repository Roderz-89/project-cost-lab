Why extension £ per m² is a blunt tool

Last checked 14 Oct 2026

The headline rate and what it hides

This lab’s own data puts single-storey rear and side-return extensions at compiled mids of about £2,650–£2,800 per m², with a low-to-high band of roughly £1,855–£3,640 per m² before specification, region, access and VAT (see the methodology). That spread is wide for a reason: glazing ratio, kitchen fit-out, drainage route, ground conditions and steelwork move the job more than the floor area alone. A cheap-looking £/m² on a simple brick box is not transferable to a highly glazed wraparound on made-up ground.

Why the floor area can mislead

An extension has fixed costs which do not shrink just because the room is small. Setting out, breaking through the existing wall, connecting services, arranging inspections and protecting the house all take time. A 10 m² addition can therefore show a higher £/m² than a 30 m² addition, even when the specification is similar.

Foundations are a major unknown. Good ground and straightforward strip footings are not the same as made-up ground, nearby trees, a sloping site or a drainage easement. The calculator can hold an allowance, but it cannot inspect the soil or promise that an engineer will accept a particular foundation.

Glazing changes the structure as well as the finish. A modest window, a rooflight and a large bi-fold opening are not interchangeable. Removing wall at the back of a house can require steelwork, temporary propping, making good and upgraded lintels.

Kitchens and party walls are often missed. Cabinets, worktops, appliances, ventilation and new circuits can make a kitchen-led extension behave more like a fit-out project than an empty shell. A side return or a build close to a boundary may also involve notices, awards, surveyors, protection and neighbour liaison. These items can be modest or material; either way, they should be visible in the budget rather than hidden inside a misleading area average.

Compiled mid points in this lab (before factors)

  • Rear single-storey — about £2,650 / m²
  • Side return — about £2,800 / m²
  • Two-storey — about £2,300 / m² (foundations and some structure shared across floors — still not “cheap”, just a different geometry)
  • Wraparound — about £2,750 / m²

These are compiled mid illustrations before specification, region, access, VAT display, contingency and extras. They are planning bands, not contractor prices.

Worked example. A mid-spec single-storey rear extension of 20 m² internal floor uses this lab’s compiled mid of about £2,650 per m², so roughly £53,000 before region, access, VAT, contingency and extras (about £37,100–£68,900 at a ±30% spread). At 20% VAT the mid becomes about £63,600; adding 10% contingency takes it to about £69,960. Inner London or a tight terrace lifts the band; a bi-fold opening, steel or a new kitchen are separate extras.

Single-storey, two-storey, rear or wraparound?

A single-storey rear extension usually has a simpler vertical build-up, but it still needs a roof, foundations and a large connection to the house. A two-storey extension adds a floor, stairs or circulation decisions, more wall and roof area, and more design considerations. Its cost per m² is not automatically double: some foundations, scaffolding, drainage work and the connection to the existing building are shared. Nor is it automatically cheaper. The useful comparison is the total scope and specification, not one headline multiplier.

A rear extension tends to have a more regular footprint. A side return uses a narrow strip that can be awkward to excavate, insulate and move materials through. A wraparound combines rear and side work, often with more corners, more roof junctions and a larger change to the ground-floor plan. It may deliver better space, but it also gives more opportunities for steel, drainage, temporary works and bespoke detailing to affect the total. See the loft conversion guide for a reminder that changing the shape and access of a project can matter as much as its new floor area.

Limits of pricing by floor area

Openings and glass. Bi-folds, roof lanterns and corner glazing change both materials and structural steel. Two extensions with the same footprint can sit in different thirds of the band.

Kitchen and services. Moving a kitchen into the extension, upgrading electrics or relocating boilers is often a large share of the lived cost — and may be omitted from a bare shell £/m².

Ground and drainage. Soft ground, trees, shared drains and pumped solutions are classic overruns. Optional extras in the calculator are allowances only.

Party walls and neighbours. Awards, surveyors and access scaffolding sit outside a simple area rate unless you add them deliberately.

Region and access still apply

Inner London in this model is illustrated at +30% on the works mid; Outer London +22%; South East outside London +12%, with lower factors further north and in the devolved nations. Tight terraces without side access add another uplift. See the region & access guide. None of these multipliers is a postcode price book.

VAT on an extension

Building an extension to an existing home is normally standard-rated (20%), and insulation built into the extension is part of that same 20% supply under HMRC’s VAT Notice 708/6. The calculator’s VAT control shows the band at the rate you choose. Read the VAT on works guide and confirm with your accountant or contractor.

Practical way to use a £/m² band

  1. Measure the proposed internal floor area of the extension, not the whole house.
  2. Start from the matching form (rear, side, two-storey, wraparound).
  3. Set spec to match finishes and glazing honestly.
  4. Tick kitchen, bi-folds, steel, underfloor heat, planning and fees only if they apply.
  5. Compare two or three written quotes on the same drawing — not against a blog mid point.

The calculator is most useful when its knobs describe decisions you have actually made. Choose the form first, then use the specification setting to reflect ordinary, better or higher-end finishes rather than to force a preferred answer. Apply the region and access choices when the site is expensive to reach, has a tight terrace or needs longer material handling. Add kitchen, glazing, steel, underfloor heating, planning and professional-fee allowances only where they belong. Keep contingency separate in your thinking: it is protection against uncertainty, not a substitute for a survey, structural design or a quote.

Use the result as a range for a conversation with a designer or builder. Record whether figures are shown including or excluding VAT, what is included in the floor area, and whether demolition, making good, decorating, appliances, landscaping and finance are excluded. Then ask each tenderer to price the same information. A narrower quote is not necessarily a better quote if it quietly leaves out the awkward work.

Extension cost FAQ

Is £/m² suitable for a first budget? Yes, as a planning signal, provided you use a range and list exclusions. It is not a valuation or a tender.

Why can a small extension cost more per m²? Fixed preliminaries, the opening into the house, kitchen connections and professional fees are spread over fewer square metres.

Does a two-storey extension cost less per m²? It can share some ground and access costs, but extra structure, floors, stairs, design and finishes may offset that benefit.

Can the calculator price my foundations or VAT? No. Its allowances and VAT control help display a planning range; they do not replace a ground assessment or determine HMRC treatment. Read the VAT guide and confirm the position for your project.
