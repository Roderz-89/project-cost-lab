Cost per square metre — what £/m² tells you about a UK home project, and what it hides

Last reviewed 27 Oct 2026 · Published by Rodway Labs

Not a quote. Every figure on this page is a planning range from the Project Cost Lab model, not a price for your house. Rodway Labs is not a contractor, architect or quantity surveyor — see the disclaimer.

Why everyone asks for a price per square metre

“How much per square metre?” is usually the first question anyone asks about an extension, a loft or a new driveway. It is a sensible question. Floor area is easy to measure, it is the same on every drawing, and a rate per m² lets you sense-check a big number in your head: a 20 m² room at £2,500 a square metre is about £50,000 before anything else goes on top.

The trouble is that a £/m² figure is a ratio, and ratios hide what sits on either side of them. A rate quoted for one job can include VAT, fees and a kitchen; a rate quoted for another can be the bare shell with nothing else. A small room usually costs more per m² than a big one, a high specification can cost well over a third more than a mid one, and the same drawing costs more in inner London than in the North East. This guide shows the per-m² bands inside the Project Cost Lab calculator, explains each of the things that make a headline rate misleading, and works through one example with the calculator’s own numbers so you can see how a “£2,650 per m²” job becomes a much bigger figure per m² once the real-world lines are added.

All the rates below come from the calculator’s model: the compiled planning ranges in its data file, last compiled in September 2026. They are not taken from a survey, a tender index or a published price book, and no outside statistics are used on this page. Treat them as a way to size a conversation with builders, not as what any one builder will charge.

Which square metre? Check the unit first

Before you compare two £/m² figures, check that they are dividing by the same area. The calculator itself uses different areas for different projects, because that is how each kind of job is normally described:

  • Lofts are measured in m² of useful floor — broadly the area you can stand and use, not the whole loft floor under the eaves.
  • Single-storey, side and wraparound extensions use m² of internal floor area. A two-storey extension uses total new floor across both levels.
  • Roofing is per m² of roof slope, which is larger than the floor below it. Render is per m² of elevation and external wall insulation per m² of wall.
  • Whole-house refurbishments, rewiring, replumbing and decorating use m² of internal floor across the whole house.

Internal area, gross external area and “useful” area can differ by a lot on the same building. Wall thickness alone takes a meaningful slice off an extension’s footprint, and a loft can lose a large share of its floor to low headroom. A builder quoting £/m² on the external footprint and a guide quoting on internal area are not comparable until you convert one to the other. If a quote gives a rate, ask which area it is measured on.

Where a £/m² figure misleads

Each of the following is a reason that a real budget, divided by the floor area, will not match a headline rate. Most of them push the figure up.

Fixed costs that do not shrink with the room

Some costs barely change whether the room is 10 m² or 40 m². Drawings and a planning application, Party Wall notices, a staircase into a loft, an ensuite’s worth of sanitaryware, a new drainage connection or a building control fee are all closer to fixed sums than to a rate per m². The calculator handles these as separate allowances you tick, rather than hiding them in the per-m² rate. For example, the householder planning and drawings allowance has a mid of £2,200, a Party Wall allowance for one adjoining owner £1,400, a proper loft staircase £3,500 and a shower room or ensuite in the new space £8,500, each before specification and region factors.

Spread a fixed sum over fewer square metres and the rate per m² goes up. That is pure arithmetic, and the worked example below shows it with the calculator’s numbers.

The small-job premium

The calculator’s area model is linear: within each project’s minimum and maximum size it charges the same rate for every square metre, so 10 m² of extension costs exactly half of 20 m² before extras. Real small jobs rarely behave like that. A builder still has to set up the site, bring in a skip and scaffold, book trades for short visits, and form the same connection to the existing house, whether the new room is small or large. Some of those costs are close to fixed, so they weigh more heavily on a small floor area.

The model does not add a separate small-job premium, and we would rather say so than imply it does. What it does do is stop you entering sizes below each project’s minimum — 8 m² for a single-storey rear extension, 10 m² for a Velux loft — and it keeps fixed items such as planning and Party Wall as lump-sum extras. If your project is near the bottom of its size range, read the high end of the band rather than the mid, and expect written quotes per m² to come in above a larger job’s.

Specification

The specification setting moves every square metre. The calculator uses three levels: budget at 0.78 of the mid rate, mid at 1.00 and high at 1.38. On a single-storey extension that is the difference between about £2,067, £2,650 and £3,657 per m² of base works. “High spec” can mean very different things — large glazed openings, structural glass, a premium roof finish, expensive flooring — so a high-spec rate from one project is a poor guide to another. Agree what “mid” means for your job before you compare rates.

Region and access

The same drawing costs more where labour and logistics cost more, and more again where materials have to come through the house. The calculator uses regional factors from 0.88 in the North East to 1.30 in inner London, and access factors of 1.00 for easy access, 1.06 for a typical suburban plot and 1.16 for a tight terrace with no side access. The factors multiply rather than add. The region and access guide lists every factor and shows the same extension priced across the UK. The short version for £/m²: never compare a London rate with a northern rate as if they described the same job.

VAT

Many quoted rates are ex-VAT; many budgets are not. For most work on an existing home, VAT is charged at the standard 20%, which is a fifth on top of an ex-VAT rate. Some work is treated differently. HMRC’s VAT Notice 708/6 (checked 27 Oct 2026) says the installation of qualifying energy-saving materials in residential accommodation, including insulation, is zero-rated until 31 March 2027 and reverts to the reduced rate of 5% from 1 April 2027. The same notice gives the example that insulating the walls and roof of a new extension as part of building it is one standard-rated supply, so an extension does not become cheaper per m² because it is well insulated.

In the calculator, a 40 m² loft insulation top-up at the default settings is about £1,088 with 20% VAT and about £907 at 0%. The VAT control is a display choice in the model, not a ruling on your job; the VAT guide explains which rate is likely and why you should confirm it on the quote.

Contingency

A contingency is money you expect to spend on things nobody can see yet: rotten timbers, poor ground, a drain in the wrong place, a decision you change halfway through. The calculator’s slider runs from 0% to 25% and starts at 10%, and it is applied last, on top of VAT. A contractor’s rate per m² will almost never include your contingency, because it is your money, not theirs. If you compare a £/m² quote with your budget divided by the floor area, take the contingency out first or you will think the quote is cheaper than it is.

What the rate includes

Finally, a per-m² rate describes a scope, and scopes differ. In the calculator, a single-storey rear extension includes groundworks, structure, roof, openings, electrics, plaster and decoration, but not kitchen units or professional fees unless you tick them. A kitchen fit-out in the new space has its own allowance of £16,000 at mid before factors. A rate quoted by someone else may include a kitchen, bi-fold doors and landscaping — or none of them. Before comparing, list what is in and out.

Worked example: a 20 m² single-storey rear extension

Take a single-storey rear extension of 20 m² internal floor area. Use the calculator’s default settings: UK mid-range region, mid specification, a typical suburban plot and living in during the works, with 20% VAT and 10% contingency. Tick three extras that most extensions need: the householder planning and drawings allowance, a Party Wall allowance for one neighbour, and architect plus structural engineer fees.

  1. Base works. 20 m² × £2,650 = £53,000. This is the headline “£2,650 per m²”.
  2. Job factors. Typical access (× 1.06) and living in (× 1.08) make the works mid £60,674. The UK mid-range region factor is 1.00, so it stays at £60,674.
  3. Extras. Planning and drawings £2,200, Party Wall £1,400, and fees at 10% of the works, £6,067. The ex-VAT subtotal is £70,342.
  4. VAT. 20% adds £14,068, giving £84,410.
  5. Contingency. 10% adds £8,441, so the illustrated mid is £92,851, with a band of about £64,903 to £120,799.

Divide the mid by 20 m² and the all-in figure is about £4,643 per m² — roughly 1.75 times the £2,650 headline rate, without any change to the size or the specification. The band works out at about £3,245 to £6,040 per m². None of that is the model being inconsistent; it is the difference between a works rate and a household budget.

Now change the size and keep everything else the same. The planning and Party Wall allowances stay fixed, so the rate per m² moves:

When £/m² is the wrong unit

Floor area is a good unit when the cost really does grow with floor area — new rooms, whole-house refurbishments, flooring, decorating. For plenty of jobs it is the wrong unit, and the calculator does not use it:

  • Kitchens are priced per linear metre of units. The model’s kitchen refit is a mid of £2,600 per metre of run before factors; a 6 m run at the default settings is about £23,574. A large kitchen with a short run of units can cost less than a small one packed with tall units and a long worktop, so a kitchen “per m²” figure tells you very little.
  • Windows and doors are priced per unit. Two rooms with the same floor area can have very different amounts of glazing.
  • Boilers, heat pumps, EV chargers and consumer units are whole-job lump sums. A heat pump’s cost depends on the house’s heat loss, emitters and pipework more than its floor area; the boiler, heat pump and PV guide covers those, and the boiler vs heat pump comparison works through one.
  • Solar PV and batteries are priced by capacity, per kWp of panels or per kWh of usable storage.
  • Fencing, guttering, fascias and damp-proofing follow a length, in linear metres.
  • Knocking through a load-bearing wall is a lump sum that scales with the width of the opening, not the area of the room you create.

Two area-priced jobs deserve care too. Bathrooms, ensuites and wet rooms are modelled per m², but a room’s sanitaryware, tiling and ventilation are partly fixed; a 3 m² shower room is not simply three-fifths of a 5 m² bathroom in practice. And a basement conversion is priced per m² of an existing cellar, but water, structure and access can dominate its cost in ways no area rate predicts. For both, read the high end of the band and get a site-specific quote early.

How to use £/m² without being misled

  1. Check the area. Internal, external, useful floor or total across floors — make sure every rate you compare uses the same one.
  2. Check the scope. Ask what the rate includes: fees, planning, Party Wall, kitchen, glazing, landscaping, decoration.
  3. Check VAT. Convert every rate to the same basis, with or without VAT, before you compare.
  4. Add fixed sums separately. Use a rate for the part that grows with area and add lump sums for the rest, as the calculator does.
  5. Adjust for size. On a small job, expect a higher rate per m² than a big one and read the top of the band.
  6. Set region, access and specification honestly. Then keep contingency as your own line, not the builder’s.
  7. Get written quotes. A £/m² band frames the conversation. Only a written quote on a defined scope prices the job.

Cost per m² FAQ

What is a typical cost per m² for an extension in the UK?

In the calculator’s model, a single-storey rear extension has a base works band of about £1,855 to £3,445 per m² of internal floor, with a mid of £2,650, before access, occupation, VAT, contingency and extras. With the default settings and planning, Party Wall and fees ticked, a 20 m² run comes out at about £4,643 per m² all-in. These are planning ranges from the model, not survey data or a quote.

Why is a small extension more expensive per m²?

Because some costs are close to fixed — drawings, planning, Party Wall, setting up the site and forming the connection to the house — and they are spread over fewer square metres. In the calculator, keeping the same extras, a 10 m² extension comes out at about £4,880 per m² against about £4,524 for 40 m². The model has no separate small-job premium, so real quotes for very small jobs may be higher still.

Do £/m² figures include VAT?

Not always, so check. Most work on an existing home is standard-rated at 20%. Installing qualifying energy-saving materials such as insulation in a home is zero-rated until 31 March 2027 and 5% from 1 April 2027, according to HMRC’s VAT Notice 708/6, checked 27 Oct 2026. The base rates on this page are ex-VAT.

Should I include contingency in a cost per m²?

Keep it separate. A contingency is money for unknowns that you hold, not part of a builder’s rate. The calculator applies it last, at 10% by default, so you can see the budget with and without it.

Can I price a kitchen per m²?

Not reliably. Kitchen cost follows the length and type of units, worktops and appliances more than floor area, so the calculator prices a kitchen refit per linear metre of run instead.

A £/m² figure is a useful first sense-check and a poor final answer.
